How AI-native companies organize work

When AI agents can do much of a young company's work, what do founders hand to agents, what stays with people, and how does that change as the company grows?

In progressGuankai Zhai, Kathleen M. Eisenhardt and Chuck EesleyUnited States and China

The questionStudy designStatus

The question

In progress

Generative AI lets a small team do work that once needed many people. Most research on AI and work studies single tasks or large firms adopting new tools. Much less is known about companies built around AI from the start.

We ask how managing AI agents differs from managing people. When agents pick up part of the work, founders still decide what to delegate, how the work is checked and who answers for it. We follow how those decisions change as a company hires, grows, and adds or drops AI applications.

Between tool and colleague

In some companies people do the work and use AI as a tool. In others, agents pick up whole pieces of work and people step in for exceptions. We trace how and why companies move between the two, in their own operations and in the products they sell.

Study design

Companies
Young companies, most founded in 2023 or later, with about 2 to 20 people
Work
Most of the work, or most of the value added, happens at a computer
Places
United States and China
Method
Comparative case studies for building theory
  • The company's story, in order. Founders walk through the company's history in three-month periods: hires, roles left unfilled and departures, AI applications added or dropped, what worked, what surprised them and what they would change.
  • Over time. Public material on each company sits alongside the interviews, and follow-up conversations months apart extend each case as it unfolds.
  • Across cases. Each case is written up as a story, and the cases are then compared to explain why companies organize work differently.

Status

The study design and interview protocol are being finalized. No findings are reported here.

A multi-case study by Guankai Zhai, Kathleen M. Eisenhardt and Chuck Eesley, Department of Management Science & Engineering, Stanford University.
